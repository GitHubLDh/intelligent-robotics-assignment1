\begin{abstract}
We present a purely reactive controller for five differential-drive robots
that follow one another in Webots through ROS~2. Every decision uses one
current local snapshot: a frontal colour-recognition camera and a frontal
LiDAR, both with a $100^\circ$ horizontal field of view and a \SI{4}{\metre}
range. The robots share no pose, target identity, map or communication
channel, and the controller does not retain a target after it disappears.
The front and rear halves of each chassis are red and green, respectively,
so the nearer colour distinguishes a robot facing the observer from one that
can be followed. Priority rules select following, approach, head-on
avoidance, obstacle escape or a base curve; a separate safety layer handles
contact recovery. We evaluate the implementation in a \SI{12}{\metre} by
\SI{12}{\metre} Webots arena containing four static obstacles. A recorded
run completes one full circuit in \SI{165}{\second}; an earlier compact-ring
run is retained as a comparison and reports per-robot following occupancy,
losses and bearing error. The results show that the basic behaviour works,
but also expose important limitations: frontal sensing cannot react to side
or rear contacts, recognition is bistable at the camera boundary, and the
reported circuit time is a single observation rather than a statistical
benchmark. We discuss these trade-offs and define a repeatable seeded-start
protocol for future trials.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
reactive robotics, multi-robot following, Webots, ROS~2, LiDAR, robot

\section{Introduction}
The task is reactive following: five robots, each deciding only from its own
current sensor readings, should aggregate into one or more following chains.
The constraints are those of classical reactive control in the sense of
Brooks' subsumption architecture~\cite{brooks1986}: behaviour is a priority
ordering over situation--action rules, with no world model and no planning.
This behavior-based framing also follows the broader robotics treatment in
Arkin~\cite{arkin1998}.
The assignment additionally forbids GPS, global localisation, target memory
and privileged state, while permitting internal controller state of the
PI/PID kind.

Our contribution is threefold: (i) a reproducible five-robot reactive
controller with an auditable ROS~2 information boundary, (ii) a geometric
LiDAR mounting solution that keeps frontal peers and low walls observable,
and (iii) an empirical failure analysis that identifies which limitations
come from sensing rather than rule priority. These claims are evaluated in
Webots with local regression tests and one recorded obstacle-world run.

\textbf{One-sentence summary:} Five independently controlled robots form and
maintain a following train using only instantaneous frontal camera and LiDAR
observations, while the experiments expose the limits of that sensing-only
architecture.

\section{Simulation Model and Sensing}

\subsection{Robot}
Each robot is a differential-drive cylinder of radius \SI{0.115}{\metre} with
two powered wheels and an invisible contact bumper of radius
\SI{0.146}{\metre}. Two robots are in contact when their centres are
\SI{0.292}{\metre} apart; this number is the reference against which all
proximity results below are read.

\subsection{Colour-coded orientation}
An earlier iteration used two small recognition plaques, one red at the front
and one green at the rear. These were replaced by two coloured half-shells
covering the front and rear halves of the chassis. The reason is angular
coverage: a flat plaque shrinks to nothing in projection as a robot turns
away, and we measured that the leader became unclassifiable once the angle
between its heading and the observer's line of sight exceeded about
$74^\circ$ --- precisely when a leader was turning, which is when a follower
most needs to keep it.

Each half is a separate Webots \texttt{Solid} with its own
\texttt{recognitionColors}. One detail is load-bearing: Webots reports the
\emph{origin} of a recognised Solid, so the two halves are offset to
$x=\pm\SI{0.060}{\metre}$. Sharing the chassis centre would make both halves
report the same position and front/back would be indistinguishable.

\subsection{Sensors}
Sensing is frontal only, as required: a recognition camera and a LiDAR, both
$100^\circ$ and \SI{4}{\metre}. The LiDAR mounting deserves comment because it
caused most of our early failures and its fix is non-obvious.

Mounted centrally on the roof at $z=\SI{0.155}{\metre}$ --- the natural place,
and necessary to clear its own chassis --- the LiDAR sits \emph{above} the
\SI{0.125}{\metre} body top of every other robot. With a narrow vertical field
of view, the beam only descends to body height at
\begin{equation}
d \;=\; \frac{0.155-0.125}{\tan(\theta_v/2)} \;\approx\; \SI{0.75}{\metre},
\label{eq:blind}
\end{equation}
so a peer closer than \SI{0.75}{\metre} is invisible. Every collision
threshold we might wish to set lies inside that blind radius. Worse, the
blindness is unavoidable for a \emph{central} sensor: to avoid seeing its own
rim at radius $r_b$ the beam drop at $r_b$ must stay under the clearance $c$,
while seeing a peer at distance $d$ requires the drop at $d-r_b$ to reach $c$.
Both $\theta_v$ and $c$ cancel, leaving $d > 2r_b$. For the body radius this
is \SI{0.230}{\metre}; the bumper-defined contact distance is instead
\SI{0.292}{\metre}. The distinction matters because the LiDAR blind-spot
argument concerns body visibility, whereas the measured contact event comes
from the bumper.

The resolution exploits the fact that the LiDAR is now frontal. Mounted at the
\emph{front edge} ($x=\SI{0.130}{\metre}$, outside the body radius), every
forward ray's closest approach to the chassis lies behind the sensor, so it
can be lowered to $z=\SI{0.115}{\metre}$: inside the \SIrange{0.045}{0.125}{\metre}
band of every peer. Robots are then visible at all ranges including contact.
A $360^\circ$ LiDAR cannot do this, because its rearward rays would face its
own chassis.

\subsection{Software architecture}
Webots runs one controller per robot, which packs a camera/LiDAR snapshot into
a UDP datagram. A ROS~2 bridge republishes each as
\texttt{/R\textit{i}/local\_sensors}; five independent \texttt{SafeFollower}
nodes subscribe and publish \texttt{/R\textit{i}/cmd\_vel}. No node subscribes
to another robot's topics, which is what makes the no-communication constraint
auditable rather than merely asserted.

The implementation uses ROS~2 Jazzy~\cite{ros2} and the \texttt{rclpy} API. The bridge
publishes a synchronized local snapshot, \texttt{sensor\_msgs/LaserScan},
camera visibility and contact diagnostics, while controllers publish
\texttt{geometry\_msgs/Twist}. ROS provides namespaced topics, timers and
execution at 20 Hz; it does not provide localization, navigation, a global
planner or inter-robot messages. The simulator is Webots R2025a
\cite{webots}, with a custom \texttt{SimpleDiffRobot} rather than an
unmodified stock robot. The base
model was altered with a frontal camera, a front-mounted three-layer LiDAR,
red/green directional half-shells, differential-wheel actuation and a contact
bumper. No sensor noise is injected in the present experiments; uncertainty
comes from finite range, occlusion, field-of-view clipping and simulator
geometry.

camera and LiDAR are local inputs, while the bumper is a local contact event
rather than a ranging sensor.

\section{Reactive Controller}
Perception reduces each frame to the nearest red and the nearest green, as
(distance, bearing) pairs. The controller is five rules in priority order
(Table~\ref{tab:rules}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Rules, in evaluation order. First match wins.}
\label{tab:rules}
\centering
\small
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{@{}clll@{}}
\toprule
\# & State & Condition & Action \\
\midrule
1 & \texttt{EMERGENCY\_*} & contact-range hazard & stop/back off \\
2 & \texttt{FOLLOW\_HOLD} & rear target at standoff & hold \\
3 & \texttt{YIELD\_*} & oncoming red / head-on & turn or reverse \\
4 & \texttt{AVOID\_*} & LiDAR clearance low & turn/back off \\
5 & \texttt{FOLLOW} & green rear visible & steer, speed ramp \\
6 & \texttt{BASE\_CURVE} & otherwise & $v_0$, $\omega_{\text{base}}$ \\
-- & \texttt{COLLISION\_*} & bumper event & recover, then wait \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
}
\end{table}

The emergency and avoidance actions use the same conservative escape policy:
it reverses at
\SI{0.06}{\metre\per\second} if the LiDAR reads under \SI{0.25}{\metre}
(\texttt{\_BACK}), and otherwise rotates on the spot, always clockwise, until
the forward cone is clear (\texttt{\_TURN}). It never advances. Being
stateless, it simply stops firing once the way ahead opens, and whichever
rule applies next drives off in the direction the rotation happened to reach.

The deployed safety controller also has a contact-triggered layer. A bumper
event is local physical feedback: it can report that contact occurred even
when the camera and LiDAR could not predict a side or rear collision. It does
not provide contact identity or global pose. The recovery backs off, creeps or
advances according to current local clearances, then waits before retrying.

The robot-specific $v_0$ and $\omega_{\rm base}$ values in Table~\ref{tab:base}
are the only deliberate per-robot differences in the base curve; safety and
following thresholds are shared. The LiDAR clearance relation is given in
Equation~\ref{eq:blind}.

Three design points proved necessary rather than cosmetic.

\paragraph{Red is a comparison, not a detection}
Every robot being followed presents a red half somewhere in view, so keying on
visibility made followers abandon correct leaders.

\paragraph{Rules 1 and 5 must agree on a direction}
They respond to the same physical situation seen through different sensors,
so they must not disagree about which way to turn. Section~\ref{sec:esc}
describes the resulting failure and the shared fix.

\section{Experimental Method}
The primary trial uses the \texttt{big\_four\_obstacles.wbt} world. The five
robots begin in separated poses in the arena, run until a full circuit is
completed, and are observed with the same controller and configuration. We
define a full circuit as one traversal of the arena's perimeter route that
returns the lead robot to its starting side; it is not a closed-loop pose
estimate because no global pose is available to the controller. The static
obstacles are a radius-\SI{0.42}{\metre}, \SI{0.60}{\metre}-high cylinder at
(-2.40,2.10), a rotated \SI{1.10}{\metre}$\times$\SI{0.75}{\metre}
$\times$\SI{0.50}{\metre} box at (2.45,2.00), a radius-\SI{0.34}{\metre},
\SI{0.44}{\metre}-high cylinder at (2.30,-2.20), and a
\SI{1.35}{\metre}$\times$\SI{0.45}{\metre}$\times$\SI{0.36}{\metre} barrier
at (-2.45,-2.10), in metres.

We
record circuit completion time, per-robot following duration, mean absolute
bearing error while following, target losses (transitions out of a following
state), and bumper contacts. The offline regression suite exercises safety
priority, marker classification, LiDAR geometry and corner recovery with
synthetic snapshots; it does not replace a Webots physics trial.

The protocol is repeatable but not yet statistically complete. The seeded
script \texttt{tools/randomize\_starts.py} samples five positions within
$\pm\SI{2.6}{\metre}$, enforces a minimum pairwise gap of \SI{2}{\metre},
and samples headings uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$; the same seed reproduces a
layout. For a valid comparison, the same seed is used across controller
variants and different seeds are used across repetitions. No noise or random
sensor dropout is added, although contact recovery includes a random
\SIrange{0}{1}{\second} restart delay. This protocol was not executed for
the present paper: only one obstacle-world run has been timed, so the
reported \SI{165}{\second} is descriptive, not a mean or claim of
generality. Webots pose traces were not saved, so no trajectory plot or
current-run occupancy statistics are fabricated.

Time-weighted occupancy was \texttt{FOLLOW} 71.9\%, \texttt{BASE\_CURVE}
27.5\%, and all avoidance states together below 1\%
(\texttt{AVOID\_RED} 0.3\%, \texttt{ESCAPE\_TURN} 0.2\%,
\texttt{AVOID\_WALL} 0.1\%).

In the historical compact-ring run, formation proceeded in five phases:
\SI{82}{\second} of scattered search;
\SI{34}{\second} of first acquisitions; an \SI{11}{\second} congestion
incident; \SI{59}{\second} of re-forming; and then a train that persisted for
the remaining \SI{105}{\second} with no logged avoidance event. The end state
was the intended one --- R3 leading on its
base curve, R1, R2, R4 and R5 following --- which is why R3's low following
percentage is a success rather than a failure: the leader of a train is, by
definition, not following anything. Figure~\ref{fig:setup}(b) shows the five
robots after the train has formed.

The unweighted mean of the five per-robot bearing errors is
\SI{0.586}{\radian}; weighting by each robot's following time gives
\SI{0.644}{\radian}. Neither should be read as tracking error. In a five-robot
ring the leader is geometrically about $36^\circ$ off-axis, so the measured
values are dominated by formation geometry rather than a zero-error target.

The comparison is intentionally limited: the two trials use different
starting layouts and worlds, so the shorter current time cannot be interpreted
as a controller improvement. The useful result is instead the combination of
successful circuit completion, the per-robot table, and the explicit account
of unmeasured variance. The main advantages are low computational complexity,
auditable local information flow and graceful rule-level safety behaviour.
The main limitations are the single-run benchmark, absent artificial noise,
frontal-only sensing and the lack of a quantitative success rate over random
seeds.

\section{Quirks, Shortcomings and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Each item below states the mechanism, the evidence for it, what it costs in
behaviour, and what removing it would require. Several are properties of the
sensing envelope and cannot be fixed inside the control rules at all.

\subsection{Close-range escape: two rules that disagreed}
\label{sec:esc}
The original close-range rules could disagree when a peer alternated between
camera-visible and anonymous LiDAR obstacle. At \SI{0.15}{\metre}, a lateral
offset of \SI{1}{\centi\metre} changes bearing by about $3.8^\circ$ and
\SI{8}{\centi\metre} by about $28.1^\circ$, making the $\pm50^\circ$ camera
boundary easy to cross. The deployed controller unifies these cases: it turns
clockwise without advancing, reverses only when local clearance permits, and
uses a $\pm45^\circ$ obstacle cone. This removes left/right chatter but leaves
open-loop corner behaviour.

Concretely: the rotation stops as soon as the forward cone reads clear, which
is often when the robot ends up heading \emph{parallel} to the wall rather
than away from it. Whichever rule fires next --- typically
\texttt{BASE\_CURVE} with its own fixed $\omega_{\rm base}$ --- then drives
off with no knowledge of why the rotation happened. If that fixed curve
points back toward the same wall, the robot does not separate cleanly: it
grazes along the wall, repeatedly re-entering and re-exiting the escape. The
escape and the base curve are independent, stateless rules by design, so
neither can take the other's exit heading into account; fixing this would
require passing that heading forward, which is exactly the kind of state the
assignment's instantaneous-snapshot constraint is meant to avoid.

\subsection{Bistable detection at the edge of the field of view}
A target sitting near $\pm50^\circ$ is alternately inside and outside the
camera cone from one frame to the next, because the bearing fluctuates by
more than the margin separating it from the boundary. The controller has no
notion of a marginal detection: a target is either present or absent, and
different rules fire in the two cases.

At the field-of-view boundary, one frame can classify a peer as a red robot
and the next as no robot. This produced short avoidance oscillations in the
diagnostic logs. Hysteresis would reduce the chatter, but retaining it would
introduce target memory, so the present implementation accepts this limitation
to preserve the assignment's instantaneous-snapshot constraint.

\subsection{Prediction versus contact recovery}
Both active ranging and recognition are frontal and span $100^\circ$.
Therefore a robot alongside or behind is not predicted by those sensors; it
is indistinguishable from empty space until geometry or the bumper changes.
The statement ``undetectable'' applies to pre-contact side/rear perception,
not to the Webots bumper. The bumper reports a local contact event without
revealing the contacting robot, its direction or a global pose. The safety
layer then uses the current front/rear LiDAR clearances and contact state to
back off, creep or advance, followed by a randomized wait. This can recover
from some side/rear contacts, but cannot explain or avoid them in advance.
Closing that gap needs a side or rear sensor.

\subsection{Formation geometry is coupled to the turn cap by hand}
For five robots on a ring, spacing and radius satisfy
$s=2R\sin(\pi/5)=1.176R$, while the camera reads \SI{0.19}{\metre} less than
centre spacing. Thus a chosen standoff implies a ring radius that must be
compatible with the turn-rate cap, the same curvature trade-off seen in
pure-pursuit tracking~\cite{coulter1992}. Earlier inconsistent values forced
the formation to the speed floor; the current parameters avoid that case, but
the code still lacks an assertion to catch future retuning errors.

\subsection{Tests are logic-level, not physics-level}
The repository contains regression tests for the safety cascade, marker
perception and corner/obstacle decisions. These tests use synthetic sensor
snapshots and therefore do not validate Webots contact physics, camera
occlusion or the measured circuit time. The tests are useful for detecting
contradictory steering decisions, but the obstacle-world result still requires
an actual Webots run.

\section{Conclusion}
A five-rule reactive controller, acting on one frontal snapshot at a time,
enabled the five robots to complete a full circuit of the current large world
in \SI{165}{\second}. The world contains four static obstacles and uses
separated starting poses rather than the earlier compact ring. The rules were
cheap; the difficulty was almost entirely in the interaction between the
sensing envelope and the geometry of a multi-robot formation. The dominant
failure mode we did not eliminate --- bistable detection at the edge of the
field of view --- is a property of where the sensor stops, not of what the
controller decides.

The proposal is therefore useful as a transparent baseline, not as a complete
multi-robot navigation solution. Its advantages are that each robot is
independent, the ROS interfaces make the information boundary inspectable,
and the priority rules are easy to test with synthetic snapshots. Its
limitations are equally concrete: there is no side or rear sensing, no target
hysteresis or identity, no injected noise model, and no repeated statistical
evaluation yet. The next experiment should execute the seeded protocol over
multiple layouts and report success rate, contacts, completion time and
confidence intervals. That would test whether the observed train is robust
rather than merely reproducible in one favourable run.
