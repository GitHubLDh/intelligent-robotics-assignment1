\begin{abstract}
We present a purely reactive controller that makes five differential-drive
robots form a following chain in Webots, driven by five ROS~2 nodes. Each
robot acts on a single current snapshot from a frontal camera and LiDAR
($100^\circ$, \SI{4}{\metre}); there is no target memory, no global pose and no
inter-robot communication. Robot orientation is read directly from colour:
the front half of each chassis is red and the rear half green, so ``facing
me'' and ``facing away'' reduce to which colour is nearer. The controller is
five prioritised rules. The robots assemble into a stable train --- one
leader on its base curve and four followers --- in roughly
\SI{3}{\minute} from a scattered start, and hold it thereafter without
further avoidance events. We report following duration, angular error and
target losses, and devote the larger part of this paper to the quirks and
failure modes that remain, several of which are consequences of the sensing
envelope rather than of the control rules.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The task is reactive following: five robots, each deciding only from its own
current sensor readings, should aggregate into one or more following chains.
The constraints are those of classical reactive control in the sense of
Brooks' subsumption architecture~\cite{brooks1986}: behaviour is a priority
ordering over situation--action rules, with no world model and no planning.
The assignment additionally forbids GPS, global localisation, target memory
and privileged state, while permitting internal controller state of the
PI/PID kind.

Our contribution is deliberately small. The interesting content of this report
is not the controller, which is roughly 120 lines, but the catalogue of ways
in which a correct-looking reactive rule set misbehaves once five copies of it
interact in a shared environment. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} is therefore
the longest section, by intent.

\section{Simulation Model and Sensing}

\subsection{Robot}
Each robot is a differential-drive cylinder of radius \SI{0.115}{\metre} with
two powered wheels and an invisible contact bumper of radius
\SI{0.146}{\metre}. Two robots are in contact when their centres are
\SI{0.292}{\metre} apart; this number is the reference against which all
proximity results below are read.

\subsection{Colour-coded orientation}
An earlier iteration used two small recognition plaques, one red at the front
and one green at the rear. These were replaced by two coloured half-shells
covering the front and rear halves of the chassis. The reason is angular
coverage: a flat plaque shrinks to nothing in projection as a robot turns
away, and we measured that the leader became unclassifiable once the angle
between its heading and the observer's line of sight exceeded about
$74^\circ$ --- precisely when a leader was turning, which is when a follower
most needs to keep it.

Each half is a separate Webots \texttt{Solid} with its own
\texttt{recognitionColors}. One detail is load-bearing: Webots reports the
\emph{origin} of a recognised Solid, so the two halves are offset to
$x=\pm\SI{0.060}{\metre}$. Sharing the chassis centre would make both halves
report the same position and front/back would be indistinguishable.

\subsection{Sensors}
Sensing is frontal only, as required: a recognition camera and a LiDAR, both
$100^\circ$ and \SI{4}{\metre}. The LiDAR mounting deserves comment because it
caused most of our early failures and its fix is non-obvious.

Mounted centrally on the roof at $z=\SI{0.155}{\metre}$ --- the natural place,
and necessary to clear its own chassis --- the LiDAR sits \emph{above} the
\SI{0.125}{\metre} body top of every other robot. With a narrow vertical field
of view, the beam only descends to body height at
\begin{equation}
d \;=\; \frac{0.155-0.125}{\tan(\theta_v/2)} \;\approx\; \SI{0.75}{\metre},
\end{equation}
so a peer closer than \SI{0.75}{\metre} is invisible. Every collision
threshold we might wish to set lies inside that blind radius. Worse, the
blindness is unavoidable for a \emph{central} sensor: to avoid seeing its own
rim at radius $r_b$ the beam drop at $r_b$ must stay under the clearance $c$,
while seeing a peer at distance $d$ requires the drop at $d-r_b$ to reach $c$.
Both $\theta_v$ and $c$ cancel, leaving $d > 2r_b$ --- twice the body radius,
which is exactly the distance at which two robots already touch.

The resolution exploits the fact that the LiDAR is now frontal. Mounted at the
\emph{front edge} ($x=\SI{0.130}{\metre}$, outside the body radius), every
forward ray's closest approach to the chassis lies behind the sensor, so it
can be lowered to $z=\SI{0.115}{\metre}$: inside the \SIrange{0.045}{0.125}{\metre}
band of every peer. Robots are then visible at all ranges including contact.
A $360^\circ$ LiDAR cannot do this, because its rearward rays would face its
own chassis.

\subsection{Software architecture}
Webots runs one controller per robot, which packs a camera/LiDAR snapshot into
a UDP datagram. A ROS~2 bridge republishes each as
\texttt{/R\textit{i}/local\_sensors}; five independent \texttt{ReactiveFollower}
nodes subscribe and publish \texttt{/R\textit{i}/cmd\_vel}. No node subscribes
to another robot's topics, which is what makes the no-communication constraint
auditable rather than merely asserted.

\section{Reactive Controller}
Perception reduces each frame to the nearest red and the nearest green, as
(distance, bearing) pairs. The controller is five rules in priority order
(Table~\ref{tab:rules}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Rules, in evaluation order. First match wins.}
\label{tab:rules}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}clll@{}}
\toprule
\# & State & Condition & Action \\
\midrule
1 & \texttt{ESCAPE\_*}    & red nearer, $d_r<0.45$ & escape \\
2 & \texttt{AVOID\_RED}   & red nearer, $d_r<0.90$ & turn right \\
3 & \texttt{FOLLOW}       & green visible          & steer to it, speed ramp \\
4 & \texttt{APPROACH}     & red only, $d_r\ge0.90$ & steer to it \\
5 & \texttt{OBSTACLE\_*}  & no robot, LiDAR $<2.0$ & escape \\
-- & \texttt{BASE\_CURVE} & otherwise              & $v_0$, $\omega_{\text{base}}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The \emph{escape} shared by rules 1 and 5 reverses at
\SI{0.06}{\metre\per\second} if the LiDAR reads under \SI{0.25}{\metre}
(\texttt{\_BACK}), and otherwise rotates on the spot, always clockwise, until
the forward cone is clear (\texttt{\_TURN}). It never advances. Being
stateless, it simply stops firing once the way ahead opens, and whichever
rule applies next drives off in the direction the rotation happened to reach.

Three design points proved necessary rather than cosmetic.

\paragraph{Red is a comparison, not a detection}
Rules 1--2 require red to be \emph{nearer than green}, not merely visible.
Every robot being followed presents a red half somewhere in view, so keying on
visibility made followers abandon correct leaders.

\paragraph{Rules 1 and 5 must agree on a direction}
They respond to the same physical situation seen through different sensors,
so they must not disagree about which way to turn. Section~\ref{sec:esc}
describes what happened when they did.

\paragraph{Turn rate is capped by radius, not by a fixed rate}
Steering straight at a leader is pure pursuit, which cuts inside the leader's
path. In a five-robot ring the leader sits about \SI{0.63}{\radian} off-axis,
giving $\omega = 1.5\times0.63 = \SI{0.94}{\radian\per\second}$ and an arc
radius of $v/\omega \approx \SI{0.15}{\metre}$ against a \SI{0.95}{\metre}
ring: the formation spirals inward until it piles up. We cap by
$\omega_{\max} = v_0/R_{\min}$ rather than by a shared $\omega$, because the
robots run at different speeds (\SIrange{0.12}{0.16}{\metre\per\second}) and a
shared cap would give each a different radius and distort the ring.

\section{Experimental Method}
The five robots start evenly spaced on a \SI{0.95}{\metre} circle, each
heading along the tangent. We record per robot: following duration, mean
absolute bearing error while following, target losses (transitions out of a
following state) and bumper contacts. A seeded script
(\texttt{tools/randomize\_starts.py}) regenerates scattered starting poses so
that runs can be repeated with different positions and headings while
remaining reproducible, and a command-line flag selects the turning strength
for the no-following / weak / strong comparison. Variance across starting
configurations has not yet been quantified, so the timings below should be
read as characteristic rather than as means over a sample.

Time-weighted occupancy was \texttt{FOLLOW} 71.9\%, \texttt{BASE\_CURVE}
27.5\%, and all avoidance states together below 1\%
(\texttt{AVOID\_RED} 0.3\%, \texttt{ESCAPE\_TURN} 0.2\%,
\texttt{AVOID\_WALL} 0.1\%).

Formation proceeds in five phases: \SI{82}{\second} of scattered search;
\SI{34}{\second} of first acquisitions; an \SI{11}{\second} congestion
incident; \SI{59}{\second} of re-forming; and then a stable train, which persisted for
the remaining \SI{105}{\second} with no avoidance event at all and showed no
sign of degrading. The end state is the intended one --- R3 leading on its
base curve, R1, R2, R4 and R5 following --- which is why R3's low following
percentage is a success rather than a failure: the leader of a train is, by
definition, not following anything.

Mean absolute bearing error while following was \SI{0.549}{\radian}
($31.5^\circ$). This should not be read as tracking error. In a five-robot
ring the leader is geometrically about $36^\circ$ off-axis, so the measured
value is close to the best achievable for this formation.

\section{Quirks, Shortcomings and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Each item below states the mechanism, the evidence for it, what it costs in
behaviour, and what removing it would require. Several are properties of the
sensing envelope and cannot be fixed inside the control rules at all.

\subsection{Close-range escape: two rules that disagreed}
\label{sec:esc}
The original design had rule 1 turn clockwise and rule 5 turn toward whichever
half of the cone was more open. The two then contradicted each other whenever
a robot was close enough that the camera could not reliably classify it.

At contact the bearing to a peer is extremely sensitive to lateral offset: at
\SI{0.15}{\metre}, \SI{1}{\centi\metre} sideways is $6^\circ$ and
\SI{8}{\centi\metre} is $39^\circ$. Two robots pressed together therefore put
each other's red half near the $\pm50^\circ$ cone edge, where detection
alternates frame to frame. Logs show the consequence directly: of 53
\texttt{AVOID\_WALL} commands, none carried any camera detection, and they
interleave with \texttt{ESCAPE\_TURN} lines reading
\texttt{red=0.15@+0.86}. The pair alternated between a clockwise command and
an anticlockwise one and sat spinning against each other, faces touching.

The same rule also made a robot grind along a wall, for two further reasons:
it returned full forward speed while turning, so it drove into what it was
avoiding; and clearance was measured over only $\pm15^\circ$, so a wall met
obliquely was never ``ahead'' at all and nothing fired.

Both are now one escape, shared by rules 1 and 5, always clockwise, never
advancing, reversing when the LiDAR reads under \SI{0.25}{\metre}, and
measuring clearance over $\pm45^\circ$. We stopped trying to distinguish the
cases: a peer seen only by its wheel is indistinguishable from a wall, and
since the correct response is the same, the distinction was never needed. The
remaining weakness is that the escape is purely open-loop in direction --- it
always turns the same way, which is what guarantees two robots separate, but
also means a robot in a corner will rotate until some direction happens to
clear rather than choosing the best one.

\subsection{Bistable detection at the edge of the field of view}
A target sitting near $\pm50^\circ$ is alternately inside and outside the
camera cone from one frame to the next, because the bearing fluctuates by
more than the margin separating it from the boundary. The controller has no
notion of a marginal detection: a target is either present or absent, and
different rules fire in the two cases.

The congestion incident shows the consequence. R3 and R5 each met an oncoming
robot, turned right, and recovered in about \SI{1.4}{\second} --- the rule
behaving exactly as intended. R4 instead alternated between
\texttt{AVOID\_WALL} and \texttt{ESCAPE\_TURN} three times in
\SI{4}{\second}. Those two rules require contradictory inputs: rule~1 needs a
red robot inside \SI{0.45}{\metre}, rule~5 needs \emph{no} robot visible at
all. Both were true in alternate frames because the robot was at
$+49.2^\circ$, which is the bearing recorded when the episode finally
resolved. The robot was not confused about what to do; it was given two
different worlds to act in.

The cost is a few seconds of thrashing, and then however long the train takes
to re-form around the disturbed robot --- of the order of a minute. Suppressing it requires asymmetric
acquire/drop thresholds --- accept a target at $50^\circ$ but only discard it
past, say, $52^\circ$ --- which is a deliberate piece of remembered state and
therefore a judgement call against the brief's prohibition on target memory.
We left it in rather than quietly adding hysteresis.

\subsection{The highest-priority rule is not the fastest-acting rule}
All commands pass through an acceleration limiter before reaching the wheels,
which exists so that a target appearing at the edge of the cone cannot produce
an instantaneous full-rate swing. It is applied uniformly, including to the
emergency rule.

\texttt{ESCAPE\_TURN} commands $v=0,\ \omega=-0.80$. The log records
$v=0.12,\ \omega=+0.37$ on the tick it engages: the robot is still rolling
\emph{forward} and still turning \emph{left}, because the limiter is unwinding
whatever the previous rule had commanded. Full reversal of a turn takes of the
order of \SI{0.2}{\second}, during which the highest-priority rule in the
system is only partially in effect. The robot travels perhaps
\SI{0.02}{\metre} toward the hazard in that window --- small, but it means the
priority ordering in Table~\ref{tab:rules} describes the \emph{decision}, not
the actuation. A correct treatment would exempt rule~1 from the limiter, at
the cost of permitting one genuinely abrupt command.

\subsection{Nothing can be sensed to the side or rear}
Both sensors span $100^\circ$ forward. A robot alongside or behind is not
poorly sensed, it is \emph{unsensed}: no reading distinguishes it from empty
space. Sideways and rear-end contact are therefore undetectable in principle,
and no control rule can respond to them, because no rule has an input that
changes when they occur.

Everything we do about it is preventive rather than reactive --- the turn-rate
cap stops followers cutting across each other's paths, and the priority of
rule~3 keeps a follower locked to the robot ahead rather than swinging toward
a newcomer. These reduce the frequency of robots ending up abreast; they
cannot detect or recover from it. This is the single largest gap between the
system and a deployable follower, and closing it needs a sensor, not a rule:
either a rear camera, a side-mounted range sensor, or staggering the LiDAR
heights per robot so peers remain mutually visible through a full circle.

\subsection{Formation geometry is coupled to the turn cap by hand}
Three parameters that look independent are not. For five robots on a ring,
spacing and radius satisfy $s = 2R\sin(\pi/5) = 1.176R$, and the camera reads
\SI{0.19}{\metre} less than centre spacing because it sits \SI{0.130}{\metre}
ahead of its own centre and the green half \SI{0.060}{\metre} behind the
leader's. A chosen standoff therefore \emph{implies} a ring radius, which must
in turn be something the turn-rate cap can actually drive.

We violated this without noticing. A standoff of \SI{0.45}{\metre} implies a
\SI{0.54}{\metre} ring, while the cap refused any arc tighter than
\SI{0.90}{\metre}. The two demands are unsatisfiable simultaneously, and the
observable result was that all five robots closed to the standoff, hit the
speed floor and crawled at \SI{0.03}{\metre\per\second} --- a symptom that
looks like a control bug and is actually an inconsistent parameter set. The
present values agree, but nothing in the code checks the relationship, and
anyone retuning one of the three will reintroduce the fault. A constructor
assertion would catch it.

\subsection{The controller cannot be single-stepped}
Snapshot freshness is judged with a wall-clock timer, while the simulator
emits snapshots on simulation time. The two agree only while Webots runs at
approximately real time. Stepping the simulation tick by tick --- the natural
way to inspect a manoeuvre --- halts simulation time while wall time
continues, so every robot declares its data stale and drops to
\texttt{SENSOR\_FAILSAFE} within a second of each click.

This is not merely an inconvenience: it means the system cannot be observed in
the mode one would most want to observe it in, and a log recorded while
stepping is not comparable with one recorded while running. We produced
exactly that confusion, attributing \SI{70}{\percent} of robot-time to sensor
dropouts and concluding the simulator was running below real time; the same
configuration run freely spends \SI{0.0}{\percent} in that state. The
conclusion was wrong and the evidence had looked strong. A simulation-time
clock would remove the problem.

\subsection{Logging is event-driven, so within-state extremes are invisible}
A line is written only when a robot changes mode. A quantity that rises and
falls \emph{within} one mode leaves no trace. This is not a cosmetic issue:
it caused us to misdiagnose a collision. \texttt{APPROACH} was recorded at
$\omega=0.27$, and the next line --- a different mode --- showed $0.73$. Since
the rule that follows caps $\omega$ at $0.14$, the $0.73$ must have been
carried in, so the true peak inside \texttt{APPROACH} was at least $0.73$ and
plausibly the $1.1$ ceiling. The log showed a mild turn immediately before a
violent one.

Target range has since been added to each line, which helps, but the sampling
remains event-driven. Any statistic computed from these logs understates
within-state extremes, and the metrics in Section~V should be read with that
in mind.

\subsection{The delivered controller has no test coverage}
The repository contains ten test modules. All of them exercise the previous
\texttt{safe\_logic} cascade and its marker-based perception, neither of which
is part of the delivered system; they import symbols that no longer exist and
would fail at collection. The current controller --- the one that produced
every result in this paper --- is covered by no automated test at all.

This matters more than it looks, because the suite was green throughout the
period when the LiDAR could not see a robot closer than \SI{0.75}{\metre}. The
tests were written against synthetic scans and agreed with every change we
made, including changes that did nothing in simulation. Passing tests were an
active source of false confidence, and we came to rely on measurements from
the simulator instead.

\section{Conclusion}
A five-rule reactive controller, acting on one frontal snapshot at a time,
assembled five robots into a stable leader-plus-four-followers train in
\SI{186}{\second} and held it without further intervention. The rules were
cheap; the difficulty was almost entirely in the interaction between the
sensing envelope and the geometry of a multi-robot formation. The dominant
failure mode we did not eliminate --- bistable detection at the edge of the
field of view --- is a property of where the sensor stops, not of what the
controller decides.
